# CashBridge v9: research basis and measured results

What changed in the models, which paper each change comes from, what it was compared with, and what the
measurement says, including where the new method did **not** win. Every number below is regenerated by
`make evidence` (`eval/reproduce_all.py`); the external dataset is downloaded and SHA-256 checked by
`scripts/fetch_external_data.py`, never shipped in the zip (its licence is unclear).

## 1. When will a buyer pay? Survival analysis instead of a two-point rule

**Problem in v8.** The 13-week forecast put each invoice either on its due date (probability *p*) or four weeks
later. That ignores early payment, ignores invoices still open at the forecast date (censoring), and
cannot learn a delay distribution.

**Method (v9, `core/payment_timing.py`).** A Kaplan–Meier estimate of the payment-delay distribution
(Kaplan & Meier 1958), on days relative to the due date from −60 (early) to +120, built **only from what was
known at the forecast date**: an invoice still unpaid that day is a censored observation, not a missing one.
For an open invoice already *d* days late, the forecast uses the conditional distribution given it has not
paid by day *d*. A per-buyer version shrinks each buyer's hazard towards the portfolio with a Beta prior
(empirical-Bayes shrinkage in the sense of Efron & Morris 1975).

**Why this family.** Appel et al. (IBM Research) show that invoice-payment prediction works from each
customer's own aggregated payment history, evaluated with time-ordered splits [1, 2]. A University of Tartu
thesis models late-payment times with survival analysis (Cox) and random survival forests because many
invoices are censored at prediction time [3].

**Measured** (`reports/forecast_backtest.md`, rolling origin: each weekly origin refits on data known that day [10]):

| Data | v8 rule | Contractual (pay on due date) | **v9 Kaplan–Meier, pooled** | Variant: per buyer, shrunk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| External: IBM accounts-receivable sample, 2,466 invoices, 100 customers, 66 origins | 69.9% | 38.1% | **20.9%** | 22.2% |
| CashBridge synthetic history, 185 invoices, 32 origins | 46.4% | 44.9% | **42.6%** | 43.1% |

(WAPE of weekly receipts over 13 weeks; lower is better.) The per-buyer variant did **not** beat the pooled
model on either dataset, and larger shrinkage was always better in time-respecting tuning, so the product
uses the pooled model and reports the variant.

**Not changed on purpose.** The locked pack figures (shortfall week 7, USD 42,831.63) still come from the
conservative v8 rule, because a lender pack must not rely on early payers. v9 is shown next to it as the
expected timing ("Receipts outlook" on the Cash page) with its own error band.

## 2. How wrong can the forecast be? Conformal intervals that are checked

**Method (`core/conformal.py`).** Split conformal prediction [4]: the 80% band for horizon *h* is the
⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉-th smallest of the absolute errors already **realised** at earlier origins (no look-ahead).
Adaptive conformal inference (ACI) [5] updates α online, α ← α + γ(α − err), to keep coverage under drift;
conformal forecasting for time series follows Stankevičiūtė, Alaa & van der Schaar [6].

**Measured (target 80%):**

| Data | Model's own Monte Carlo P10–P90 | **Split conformal (used)** | ACI |
|---|---|---|---|
| External IBM sample | 87.1% (too wide) | **80.6%** | 81.0% |
| CashBridge synthetic | 80.8% | **80.1%** | 74.0% |

ACI under-covered on the short synthetic history (about 200 evaluated points), so it is reported, not used.

## 3. Will this buyer pay on time? A model that generalises to new customers

**Method (`agents/buyer_behaviour.py`).** Point-in-time behavioural features after Appel et al. [1, 2]: the
customer's shrunk on-time rate, average days late in the last 90 days, whether the last invoice was late,
invoices open at issue, invoice size relative to the customer's median. Every feature is computed from
information available on the invoice date.

**Validation** (`reports/external_buyer_validation.md`): split by time (train 2012, test 2013) and, separately,
by customer (30% of customers never seen in training); proper scoring rules (Brier, log loss) [8],
calibration error (ECE), ROC-AUC, PR-AUC for the late class, and a 95% interval from a customer-clustered
bootstrap.

| Split | Model | Brier (95% CI) | ROC-AUC (95% CI) | ECE |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Time | v8 logistic | 0.132 (0.109–0.157) | 0.863 (0.825–0.896) | 0.071 |
| Time | **v9 behavioural** | **0.121** (0.099–0.144) | **0.872** (0.836–0.906) | **0.021** |
| Time + unseen customers | v8 logistic | 0.206 (= base rate) | 0.500 | 0.002 |
| Time + unseen customers | **v9 behavioural** | **0.116** (0.075–0.155) | **0.896** (0.811–0.948) | 0.040 |

The v8 model keys on buyer identity, so for a new customer it falls back to the base rate. v9 is shown to
the lender **next to** the v8 score as a labelled second opinion (for INV-1030: 83% vs 97%, because the invoice is
larger than this buyer's usual). It is not a credit decision.

**Leakage screen.** A column that predicts the label on its own (AUC ≥ 0.9) is flagged: it caught
`days_to_settle` (AUC 1.0; a model given it scores 0.9998, too good to be true). It would not catch a weaker
after-the-fact column such as `Disputed`; that one is excluded by design. A screen is a check, not a guarantee.

## 4. Which financing plan? Tail risk and model uncertainty

**Method (`agents/intelligence_core.py`).** v8 already chooses on expected cost + 0.02 × CVaR95 of the
shortfall (Rockafellar & Uryasev [7]). v9 adds an optional **robust** objective: each plan is also scored in
a fixed, finite ambiguity set of worlds (any one buyer's on-time odds halved; all buyers a third worse;
orders shipping at 85%) and plans are ranked by their worst-case P(goal). This is the finite-scenario special
case of distributionally robust optimisation [11, 12]; the set is policy, not fitted. Every chosen plan now
also carries a **stress test** table (the same plan in each of those worlds).

**Measured** (`reports/risk_planner.md`, 40 synthetic quarters, scored out of sample in a hidden world):

| Paired comparison | P(goal) | CVaR95 shortfall | Expected cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Risk-aware − cheapest | +0.8 pp (0.0 to +1.0) | −1,571 USD (−2,491 to −702) | +0 |
| Robust − risk-aware | +0.6 pp (0.0 to +1.0) | −922 USD | +92 USD |

The robust gain is small and its interval touches zero, so **risk-aware stays the default**; robust is a
choice in the API (`objective="robust"`), and the stress test is always shown.

## 5. Financing cost that is counted once

`core/funding_schedule.py` ties quote, drawdown and settlement together: for factoring, the advance arrives
at drawdown and each expected receipt of the pledged invoice is replaced by its share of the rebate (invoice
minus advance, fee and interest to that day), so the invoice is never counted twice in the cash plan.
Scenarios: buyer pays on time / 30 days late / never, with recourse vs non-recourse. Background on
factoring for SMEs: Klapper, World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 3593 [9]. 150 generated cases check
that the schedule reproduces the quote to the cent and that cash is conserved.

## 6. Can CashBridge say anything about an SME's own credit risk? A distress screen, validated on public data

**Data.** No Vietnamese or Chinese SME default data is public. The closest public, licensed data is the Polish Companies
Bankruptcy set (Tomczak 2016, UCI, CC BY 4.0) [15]: 43,405 company-years, 64 financial ratios, bankruptcy status 1 to 5
years later. It is downloaded from a GitHub mirror (UCI is unreachable from the build environment), checked against the
counts UCI publishes for every file, pinned by SHA-256 and never shipped. Generated ("self-built") default data was ruled
out: validating a model on data the same team generated proves nothing.

**Question.** How much of the bankruptcy signal is in the ratios CashBridge already computes from a ledger (DSO, DIO,
gross margin), compared with balance-sheet ratios? Baselines: Altman's Z'' with its published weights [16], the Altman
ratios refitted, all 64 ratios (logistic), and boosted trees on all 64 ratios as a ceiling (cf. Zięba et al. [17]).
Stratified 5-fold cross-validation per horizon, every transform fitted on training folds only, bootstrap 95% intervals
(`reports/external_distress_validation.md`).

| Years to bankruptcy status | CashBridge ledger ratios | Altman Z'' (fixed) | Altman refitted | All 64, logistic | All 64, trees |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **0.736** (0.705–0.765) | 0.763 | 0.781 | 0.864 | 0.960 |
| 2 | 0.644 | 0.697 | 0.709 | 0.786 | 0.948 |
| 3 | 0.622 | 0.690 | 0.700 | 0.770 | 0.933 |
| 4 | 0.590 | 0.645 | 0.648 | 0.747 | 0.935 |
| 5 | 0.607 | 0.688 | 0.699 | 0.778 | 0.962 |

(ROC-AUC, out of fold.) The working-capital ratios carry some signal, most of it at the 1-year horizon, but well below
models with a balance sheet. The tree ceiling is not an artefact of missing data: with missing values hidden it still
scores 0.93–0.96, although the number of missing ratios alone scores 0.63–0.73 on this dataset.

**In the product.** The 1-year ledger-ratio model is shown on the Financial health page as an external benchmark
labelled "not a credit decision", with its limitations and an extrapolation flag when a ratio is outside the training
range. The practical conclusion is that a lender-grade signal needs the SME's balance sheet, which CashBridge does not
collect yet.

**SBA loans.** The U.S. SBA 7(a) loan records (Li, Mickel & Taylor 2018) [18] are the closest public *loan-default*
data, but the file (about 170 MB) cannot be downloaded here. `eval/external_sba_validation.py` runs as soon as it is
attached: approval-time features only, train 1995–2002 / test 2003–2006, and a leakage screen on post-outcome columns
(charge-off amount and date). It has not been run; no SBA figure is claimed.

## Not claimed

- The external data are a public, anonymised IBM sample with 30-day terms only, not Vietnamese or Chinese SMEs.
- Receipts are backtested, not full cash balances (costs and new sales are not in any available dataset).
- Planner results are simulated. No real lender quote, no real user test, no real cloud deployment, no
  green-finance eligibility or certification.
